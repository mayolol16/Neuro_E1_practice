### Q151: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 6-7 & Content Q4: Phototransduction Dark Current*

An investigator records the resting intracellular potential of human rod photoreceptors in total darkness. The outer segments maintain a resting membrane potential of approximately -40 mV with steady inward sodium and calcium currents. Which molecular mechanism sustains this tonic depolarization in the dark?

- **A.** High cytoplasmic levels of cyclic GMP opening cyclic nucleotide-gated channels
- **B.** Continuous phosphorylation of transducin by active rhodopsin kinase
- **C.** Active uptake of glutamate through presynaptic sodium-dependent transporters
- **D.** Tonic inhibition of guanylyl cyclase by low intracellular calcium levels
- **E.** Voltage-gated sodium channel activation triggered by extracellular calcium

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: A**

In total darkness, retinal rod and cone photoreceptors are tonically depolarized to around -40 mV due to a continuous inward sodium and calcium influx known as the 'dark current'. Guanylyl cyclase tonically synthesizes cGMP, which binds directly to and keeps open cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) cation channels in the outer segment plasma membrane. Continuous influx of Na+ and Ca2+ depolarizes the terminal, stimulating tonic exocytosis of the neurotransmitter glutamate into the synaptic cleft.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** Darkness = high cGMP -> open CNG channels -> Na+/Ca2+ influx ('dark current') -> tonic depolarization (-40 mV) & steady glutamate release.

</details>

### Q152: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 6-7 & Content Q4: Phototransduction Cascade*

During phototransduction in a rod photoreceptor, absorption of a single photon triggers a rapid intracellular signaling cascade that leads to cellular hyperpolarization. Which molecular event represents the direct downstream consequence of transducin activation?

- **A.** Opening of voltage-gated potassium channels in the inner segment
- **B.** Direct phosphorylation of cyclic nucleotide-gated cation channels
- **C.** Phosphorylation of rhodopsin by calcium-dependent protein kinase C
- **D.** Isomerization of all-trans-retinal back into 11-cis-retinal molecules
- **E.** Activation of cGMP phosphodiesterase type 6 to hydrolyze cyclic GMP

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: E**

When a photon is absorbed by 11-cis-retinal, it photoisomerizes to all-trans-retinal, conformational changing rhodopsin into metarhodopsin II. Metarhodopsin II activates the G-protein transducin (Gt). The active alpha-subunit of transducin then binds to and activates cGMP phosphodiesterase (PDE6), which rapidly hydrolyzes cGMP to 5'-GMP. The resulting fall in cytoplasmic cGMP causes CNG channels to close, stopping the dark current and hyperpolarizing the photoreceptor.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** Photon -> 11-cis to all-trans retinal -> metarhodopsin II -> transducin (Gt) -> PDE6 activation -> decreased cGMP -> CNG channels close -> hyperpolarization.

</details>

### Q153: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 6-7 & Content Q4: Inactivation of Phototransduction*

Following exposure to a brief pulse of bright light, rod photoreceptors rapidly restore baseline dark-adapted sensitivity. Which enzymatic process is primarily responsible for terminating activated metarhodopsin II signaling during this recovery phase?

- **A.** Rhodopsin kinase phosphorylation of metarhodopsin II followed by arrestin binding
- **B.** Hydrolysis of guanosine triphosphate by the alpha subunit of transducin alone
- **C.** Cleavage of all-trans-retinal by cytosolic retinoid isomerohydrolase enzymes
- **D.** Receptor-mediated endocytosis of rhodopsin into retinal pigment epithelial lysosomes
- **E.** Dephosphorylation of cyclic nucleotide-gated channels by calcineurin phosphatase

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: A**

To terminate phototransduction and allow rapid recovery, active metarhodopsin II must be shut off. Rhodopsin kinase (G-protein-coupled receptor kinase 1, GRK1) specifically phosphorylates multiple serine/threonine residues on the C-terminus of active rhodopsin. The cytosolic protein arrestin then binds the phosphorylated receptor, sterically blocking any further transducin activation. Concurrently, transducin hydrolyzes GTP to GDP (accelerated by RGS9), and guanylyl cyclase replenishes cGMP.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** Rhodopsin inactivation requires phosphorylation by rhodopsin kinase (GRK1) + arrestin capping, preventing further Gt activation.

</details>

### Q154: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 7-8 & Content Q1: On-Center Bipolar Cell Receptors*

A neuroscientist records from retinal bipolar cells while shining a focal spot of light onto their receptive field centers. An ON-center bipolar cell responds to central light with marked membrane depolarization. Which receptor type mediates the hyperpolarizing response of this cell to glutamate in darkness?

- **A.** Ionotropic kainate receptors promoting rapid sodium and calcium influx
- **B.** Presynaptic GABAB receptors activating inward-rectifying potassium channels
- **C.** Ionotropic AMPA receptors opening non-selective monovalent cation channels
- **D.** Metabotropic glutamate receptor type 6 coupled to Gi/Go signaling proteins
- **E.** Ionotropic NMDA receptors that require glycine as an essential co-agonist

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: D**

Photoreceptors release glutamate tonically in the dark and decrease release in response to light. ON-center bipolar cells express metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR6). In the dark, glutamate binding to mGluR6 activates the G-protein Go, which closes cation channels (TRPM1), keeping the ON bipolar cell hyperpolarized. When light decreases glutamate release, this tonic inhibition is removed, allowing TRPM1 channels to open and depolarizing the ON-center cell.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** ON-center bipolar cells express mGluR6 (metabotropic): glutamate inhibits/hyperpolarizes them; light removes glutamate -> cell depolarizes.

</details>

### Q155: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 7-8 & Content Q1: Off-Center Bipolar Cell Receptors*

In contrast to ON-center cells, OFF-center bipolar cells respond to a sudden decrease in luminance (darkness in their receptive field center) with cellular depolarization and increased glutamate release onto ganglion cells. Which postsynaptic receptor mechanism explains this response?

- **A.** Metabotropic mGluR1 receptors that stimulate inositol trisphosphate synthesis
- **B.** Ionotropic AMPA and kainate receptors depolarized directly by glutamate
- **C.** Ligand-gated chloride channels opened directly by glutamate neurotransmission
- **D.** Presynaptic nicotinic receptors facilitating vesicular acetylcholine release
- **E.** Metabotropic mGluR6 receptors closing transient receptor potential channels

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: B**

OFF-center bipolar cells preserve the sign of photoreceptors using conventional ionotropic glutamate receptors (AMPA and kainate). In the dark, when photoreceptors release abundant glutamate, glutamate binds AMPA/kainate receptors on OFF-center bipolar cells, opening ligand-gated cation channels (Na+ influx) and depolarizing the cell. Light decreases glutamate, allowing OFF bipolar cells to hyperpolarize.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** OFF-center bipolar cells express AMPA/kainate (ionotropic): glutamate depolarizes them directly in the dark; light hyperpolarizes them.

</details>

### Q157: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 6-8 & Content Q1: Amacrine Cell Circuitry*

Under scotopic (starlight/dim) conditions, rod photoreceptors signal through rod bipolar cells, which do not synapse directly onto retinal ganglion cells. Instead, visual impulses pass through specialized interneurons in the inner plexiform layer. Which interneuron mediates this essential relay?

- **A.** Horizontal cells connecting rod terminals directly to retinal ganglion axons
- **B.** Retinal pigment epithelial cells transferring electrical current via gap junctions
- **C.** AII amacrine cells connecting rod bipolar cells to cone ON and OFF pathways
- **D.** Radial Muller glial cells generating calcium waves across the retinal lamina
- **E.** Centrifugal sympathetic axons modulating ganglionic dendritic branching patterns

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: C**

Under dim/scotopic conditions, rod signals travel to rod bipolar cells. Unlike cone bipolar cells, rod bipolar cells do not synapse directly onto ganglion cells. ### Q158: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 5-6 & Content Q2: Retinal Layer Blood Supply*

A 72-year-old man with severe carotid atherosclerosis has sudden painless monocular visual loss. Fundoscopy demonstrates ischemic whitening of the inner retina with a cherry-red spot at the fovea. Which vessel normally supplies the inner two-thirds of the retina affected by this infarction?

- **A.** Anterior ciliary arteries derived from muscular branches of the rectus muscles
- **B.** Supraorbital and supratrochlear terminal branches of the ophthalmic artery
- **C.** Long posterior ciliary arteries providing circumferential limbal arcade flow
- **D.** Central retinal artery supplying the ganglion and bipolar cell layers
- **E.** Short posterior ciliary arteries supplying the choroidal choriocapillaris

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: D**

The retina has a dual blood supply. The inner two-thirds (including the nerve fiber, ganglion cell, inner plexiform, and inner nuclear layers) is supplied by branches of the central retinal artery, a direct branch of the ophthalmic artery. The outer one-third (photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium) is avascular and receives oxygen and nutrients by diffusion from the underlying choroid (supplied by short posterior ciliary arteries). At the fovea, the inner layers are absent, so the choroid shines through as a 'cherry-red spot' when the surrounding ischemic inner retina whitens.

> **High-Yield Pearl:** Inner 2/3 of retina (ganglion/bipolar) = Central retinal artery. Outer 1/3 (photoreceptors/RPE) = Posterior ciliary arteries / choroid.

</details>

### Q160: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 5-6 & Content Q2: Central Retinal Vein Occlusion*

A 64-year-old man with poorly controlled hypertension and open-angle glaucoma reports sudden, painless, blurry vision in his left eye. Fundoscopy reveals extensive flame-shaped and blot hemorrhages across all four quadrants, engorged and tortuous retinal veins, cotton-wool spots, and optic disc edema. Which pathophysiologic mechanism best accounts for this 'blood and thunder' appearance?

- **A.** Atheroembolic occlusion of the main trunk of the ophthalmic artery
- **B.** Autoimmune granulomatous inflammation of the temporal arterial wall
- **C.** Thrombosis of the central retinal vein at or near the lamina cribrosa
- **D.** Separation of the neurosensory retina from the pigment epithelium
- **E.** Subretinal choroidal neovascularization penetrating Bruch's membrane

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: C**

Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) is typically caused by thrombosis of the central retinal vein where it passes through the lamina cribrosa alongside the central retinal artery. ### Q166: Visual System  
*Source: Lecture Slide 14-16 & Content Q4: LGN Magnocellular Layers*

The lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) consists of six distinct cellular layers. Layers 1 and 2 comprise the magnocellular pathway, receiving inputs from parasol (M) retinal ganglion cells. Which visual perceptual modality is predominantly processed by these two magnocellular layers?

- **A.** Detection of rapid motion, flicker, depth, and broad spatial organization
- **B.** Fine spatial acuity required for reading fine printed text and faces
- **C.** Direct initiation of autonomic circadian neuroendocrine hormone release
- **D.** High-resolution color vision in the red-green chromatic spectrum

**Correct Answer: A**

A 35-year-old woman presents with pain, redness, photophobia, and a foreign-body sensation in her left eye. Slit-lamp examination with cobalt blue illumination following fluorescein dye instillation reveals the corneal lesion shown in the exhibit: a delicate, branching dendritic ulcer with terminal end-bulbs. Which medication is strictly contraindicated because it promotes viral replication and can lead to a large geographic corneal ulcer?

![Figure](images/dendritic_cornea.svg)

- **A.** Oral antiviral therapy with acyclovir or valacyclovir tablets
- **B.** Topical cycloplegic agents such as cyclopentolate drops
- **C.** Topical ophthalmic corticosteroid drops such as prednisolone acetate
- **D.** Topical antiviral agents such as ganciclovir ophthalmic gel
- **E.** Preservative-free artificial tear lubricating eye drops

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: C**

Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1) keratitis results from viral reactivation from the trigeminal ganglion (V1). Slit-lamp exam with fluorescein staining reveals the pathognomonic branching dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs. Topical corticosteroids are strictly contraindicated in active HSV epithelial keratitis: suppressing local cell-mediated immunity allows uninhibited viral replication, transforming a small dendritic ulcer into a massive 'geographic' or 'amoeboid' ulcer that causes corneal stromal melting and perforation. Treatment requires topical or oral antivirals (ganciclovir, trifluridine, acyclovir).

> **High-Yield Pearl:** HSV keratitis: Branching dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs. TOPICAL STEROIDS STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED (causes massive geographic ulcer).

### Q210: Infection/Inflammation of eye disorders  
*Source: Content Q7 & Case 6: Anterior Uveitis with Hypopyon*

A 29-year-old man with a history of chronic inflammatory low back pain and morning stiffness (HLA-B27 ankylosing spondylitis) presents with severe, throbbing pain, redness, and marked photophobia in his right eye. Slit-lamp examination reveals pronounced perilimbal ciliary injection ('ciliary flush'), a constricted and sluggish pupil, and floating white cells in the anterior chamber that have settled into a distinct, horizontal white fluid level (hypopyon), as shown in the exhibit. What is the diagnosis and appropriate initial pharmacotherapy?

![Figure](images/eye_hypopyon.svg)

- **A.** Acute bacterial endophthalmitis; treated with immediate pars plana vitrectomy and intravitreal vancomycin
- **B.** Fungal keratitis; treated with topical natamycin and surgical debridement of the corneal stroma
- **C.** Orbital cellulitis; treated with intravenous vancomycin and ceftriaxone with surgical sinus drainage
- **D.** Acute angle-closure glaucoma; treated with topical pilocarpine and intravenous acetazolamide
- **E.** Anterior uveitis (iridocyclitis); treated with topical corticosteroids and topical cycloplegics

<details><summary>Click to view Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct Answer: E**

Anterior uveitis (iritis or iridocyclitis) is inflammation of the iris and ciliary body, frequently associated with HLA-B27 systemic spondyloarthropathies (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, IBD). Hallmark findings include severe aching eye pain, photophobia, ciliary flush (violaceous perilimbal ring of injected deep episcleral vessels), pupillary miosis (due to sphincter spasm), and leukocytes/protein flare in the anterior chamber. When white blood cells layer out dependently at the bottom of the anterior chamber, it forms a sterile hypopyon. Treatment requires topical corticosteroids (to suppress inflammation) plus topical cycloplegic/mydriatic drops (e.g., cyclopentolate, atropine) to paralyze the ciliary muscle (relieving pain) and dilate the pupil (preventing posterior synechiae).

> **High-Yield Pearl:** Anterior uveitis (HLA-B27): Ciliary flush + constricted pupil + anterior chamber cells/hypopyon. Treat: topical steroids + cycloplegics (atropine/cyclopentolate).

</details>
